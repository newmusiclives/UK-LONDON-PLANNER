What Londoners Actually Do on Sundays — And What You Should Steal for Your Trip

Published 20 April 2026 · Spring edition

Here's a secret that every London guidebook dances around: Sunday is a better day than Saturday. The pace is different. The city breathes out. Londoners themselves — the ones who've been here long enough to have opinions about which Tesco Metro has the best self-checkout ratio — will tell you their favourite day of the week is Sunday, and they'll get a bit defensive about it if pressed.

If you plan your trip so that at least one Sunday is free of scheduled attractions, you'll see London the way people who actually live here experience it. No queue for the Tower. No 8 a.m. alarm for the Changing of the Guard. Just good food, slower mornings, and the specific joy of wandering through a market with a coffee in hand and nowhere to be for hours.

Here's what that looks like, neighbourhood by neighbourhood.

Morning: The Market Crawl

London has roughly 400 markets. Most of them run on weekends. But Sunday is when the good ones peak.

Columbia Road Flower Market (Columbia Road, E2 7RG) opens at 8 a.m. and is wall-to-wall flowers, potted herbs, and vendors shouting prices that drop as noon approaches. The trick: arrive by 8:15 or after 11:30. Between 9:30 and 11, the street is so packed that you'll spend more time with your face in a stranger's shoulder bag than looking at tulips. The surrounding shops — independent ceramicists, vintage furniture, a tiny perfumery — open only on Sundays, which makes the whole street feel like a pop-up that's been running since 1869.

Broadway Market (Broadway Market, E8 4PH), a ten-minute walk south, is where Hackney feeds itself. The food stalls rotate, but regulars include excellent Ethiopian injera wraps and a sourdough pizza stand that's been turning out better pies than half the sit-down restaurants in Zone 1. Budget about £6–£10 for a solid market lunch. (All our prices are in £ — use the currency switcher in the top right if you need USD, EUR, AUD, or CAD.)

If east London isn't your thing, Maltby Street Market (Ropewalk, SE1 3PA) under the Bermondsey railway arches is smaller, calmer, and more food-focused. The cheese toasties from Kappacasein — melted raclette with onions and cornichons — have achieved a level of local fame that borders on religious. Arrive by 10 a.m.; some stalls sell out by 1 p.m.

Late Morning: The Sunday Roast

The Sunday roast is to Londoners what brunch is to New Yorkers — a weekly ritual that people plan their entire day around. You book a table, you sit down, and you eat a quantity of meat, potatoes, Yorkshire pudding, and gravy that would alarm your cardiologist.

A few places that consistently deliver:

  • The Harwood Arms (Walham Grove, SW6 1QP) — the only Michelin-starred pub in London. Their roast venison is extraordinary. Book at least two weeks ahead. Mains from about £28.
  • The Eagle (Farringdon Road, EC1R 3AL) — widely credited as London's first gastropub, opened 1991. No bookings; just turn up. Roast beef with all the trimmings for around £18. The room is loud and slightly chaotic, which is part of the charm.
  • The Bull & Last (Highgate Road, NW5 1QS) — right at the edge of Hampstead Heath, so you can walk off the damage afterwards. Their bone marrow and roast chicken are both outstanding. Mains around £22–£26.

A word on Yorkshire puddings: they should be crisp on the outside, slightly soft in the middle, and roughly the size of your fist. If a pub serves you a flat, pale disc, you are within your rights to be disappointed.

Afternoon: The Walk

After a roast, Londoners walk. It's not optional. Your body requires it.

Regent's Canal is the classic Sunday afternoon route. Pick it up at Little Venice (Warwick Avenue tube) and walk east through Regent's Park, past London Zoo's aviary, through Camden Lock — where you can stop for another coffee or, if your roast has settled, a brownie — and on towards King's Cross. The full stretch to Islington is about three miles, flat the entire way, and lined with narrowboats painted in colours that look like a children's book illustration.

If the weather's cooperating (April in London: could be 18°C and glorious, could be 7°C and horizontal rain — pack layers), Hampstead Heath offers proper hills with proper views. Climb to Parliament Hill and you get a panorama of the entire London skyline, from the Shard to the BT Tower, spread out below you like a model village. Free. Always open. Best around 4 p.m. when the light goes golden.

Late Afternoon: Free Culture

Most of London's major museums and galleries are free, and on Sunday afternoons they're noticeably quieter than Saturdays.

Tate Modern (Bankside, SE1 9TG) is open until 6 p.m. The permanent collection alone — Rothko, Dalí, Bourgeois — could fill two hours. The turbine hall installations change seasonally and are always worth a look, even if you decide after thirty seconds that you don't like them. That's a valid response to contemporary art.

The Wallace Collection (Manchester Square, W1U 3BN) is wildly underrated: a townhouse packed with Old Masters, armour, and French furniture that feels like sneaking into someone's very fancy home. Almost never crowded. Completely free.

Evening: The Pub Wind-Down

Sunday evening pubs are different from Friday evening pubs. The energy is lower. People are reading newspapers, playing board games, talking in normal-volume voices. This is when London pubs are at their best.

The Lamb and Flag (Rose Street, WC2E 9EB) in Covent Garden has been pouring pints since 1623 and is one of the few pubs in that neighbourhood that hasn't been entirely swallowed by tourist pricing. A pint will still run you about £6.50–£7.00. Get a table in the upstairs room if you can.

Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese (Fleet Street, EC4A 2BU) is down an alley off Fleet Street and looks like it hasn't been redecorated since Dr. Johnson used to drink here in the 1700s. Low ceilings, dark wood, multiple levels connected by narrow staircases. It's a bit theatrical, but it earned it.

The Honest Truth About Sunday Logistics

A few things to know:

  • Tube service: The Underground runs a normal Sunday timetable, but weekend engineering works are common. Check TfL before you head out — specific lines close for maintenance almost every Sunday.
  • Shop hours: Under UK Sunday trading laws, large shops can only open for six hours (usually 12–6 p.m.). Small shops set their own hours. Plan any shopping accordingly.
  • Restaurant bookings: Sunday roast spots fill up. Book by Wednesday for popular places.

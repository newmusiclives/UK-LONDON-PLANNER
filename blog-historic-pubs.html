Pubs & History

London's 20 Best Historic Pubs

Centuries of history in a pint glass — a pub crawl through London's most storied drinking establishments

The London pub is not just a place to drink. It is a living museum, a social institution, and a window into centuries of history. Pubs in London have survived the Great Fire of 1666, the Blitz of 1940, and the relentless march of property developers. They have hosted everyone from Charles Dickens to Karl Marx, from Samuel Johnson to the Kray twins. This guide covers 20 of the best historic pubs in London, organized as a rough pub crawl route starting in the east and working west. You could tackle it over several visits, or attempt the whole thing in one heroic day — though we would strongly recommend pacing yourself.

East London and the City

1. The Prospect of Whitby (1520)

Address: 57 Wapping Wall, E1W 3SH

London's oldest riverside pub, established in 1520 as the Devil's Tavern. It was a favourite haunt of smugglers, thieves, and pirates — a noose still hangs over the river as a reminder. Samuel Pepys, Charles Dickens, and the painter Turner all drank here. The pewter-topped bar is original, and the Thames views from the terrace are outstanding.

What to drink: A pint of real ale on the riverside terrace. The pub serves a good selection of cask ales.

2. The Town of Ramsgate (1545)

Address: 62 Wapping High Street, E1W 2PN

Just a short walk from the Prospect of Whitby, this narrow pub overlooks Wapping Old Stairs, where convicted pirates were chained at low tide and left for three tides to wash over them. Captain Blood was captured here after stealing the Crown Jewels in 1671. The pub has a tiny, atmospheric outdoor area by the river.

What to drink: London Pride or a pint of Guinness in the snug back room.

3. The Lamb Tavern (1780)

Address: 10-12 Leadenhall Market, EC3V 1LR

Set inside the stunning Victorian Leadenhall Market (which doubled as Diagon Alley in the Harry Potter films), this pub has been serving City workers since the 18th century. The ornate tiled interior with etched glass screens is original and beautifully preserved.

What to drink: A pint of Young's bitter while admiring the Victorian tile work.

4. Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese (1667)

Address: 145 Fleet Street, EC4A 2BU

Rebuilt just after the Great Fire of London in 1667, this labyrinthine pub on Fleet Street is one of the most atmospheric in London. Low ceilings, dark wood, sawdust on the floors, and a warren of rooms spread over multiple levels. Samuel Johnson, Charles Dickens, Mark Twain, and Arthur Conan Doyle all drank here. The vaulted cellar may date back to the 13th century.

What to drink: A pint of Sam Smith's — the pub is owned by Samuel Smith's brewery and their beers are notably cheaper than average London prices, around 4 pounds a pint.

5. Ye Olde Mitre (1546)

Address: 1 Ely Court, Ely Place, EC1N 6SJ

Hidden down a narrow alley between Hatton Garden and Clerkenwell Road, this pub is so well concealed that many Londoners have never found it. Originally built in 1546 for the servants of the Bishop of Ely, it sits on land that technically belonged to Cambridgeshire until 1772. A cherry tree trunk inside the pub is said to mark the boundary around which Elizabeth I danced.

What to drink: An excellent selection of real ales. Try whatever guest cask is on — the pub takes its beer seriously.

The South Bank and Southwark

6. The George Inn (1677)

Address: 75-77 Borough High Street, SE1 1NH

London's last surviving galleried coaching inn, now owned by the National Trust. The current building dates from 1677, though there has been an inn on this site since medieval times. Shakespeare almost certainly drank here — the Globe Theatre was just around the corner. Dickens mentions it in Little Dorrit. The cobbled courtyard with its wooden galleries is extraordinary.

What to drink: A pint in the courtyard on a summer evening, imagining the coaching days when this was a bustling stopping point on the road to London.

7. The Anchor Bankside (1615)

Address: 34 Park Street, SE1 9EF

Sitting on the south bank of the Thames with views of St Paul's Cathedral, this pub has stood here in various forms since the early 17th century. Samuel Pepys reportedly watched the Great Fire of London from this spot in 1666. Samuel Johnson wrote much of his famous dictionary while drinking here.

What to drink: Grab a pint and sit on the riverside terrace for one of the best pub views in London.

8. The Old Thameside Inn (est. site 16th century)

Address: Pickfords Wharf, Clink Street, SE1 9DG

Located in a converted spice warehouse on Clink Street, next to the remains of the Bishop of Winchester's Palace and the Clink Prison Museum. The area was once London's red-light district and entertainment quarter. The pub offers good views of the Golden Hinde replica and a direct connection to the area's colourful history.

What to drink: A pint of ale while looking out at the Thames and Tower Bridge.

Central London and the West End

9. The Lamb and Flag (1623)

Address: 33 Rose Street, WC2E 9EB

Tucked down a narrow alley in Covent Garden, this tiny pub dates from 1623 and was once known as the Bucket of Blood due to the bare-knuckle prize fights held here. The poet John Dryden was beaten up outside in 1679, allegedly on the orders of the Earl of Rochester. Charles Dickens was a regular.

What to drink: It gets packed in the evenings — arrive early for a spot at the bar. Good selection of ales and a decent pub food menu.

10. The French House (1891)

Address: 49 Dean Street, W1D 5BG

This small Soho pub was the unofficial headquarters of the Free French forces during World War II — Charles de Gaulle is said to have drafted his famous rallying speech here. Dylan Thomas, Francis Bacon, and Brendan Behan were regulars. The pub famously serves only half pints of beer, encouraging conversation over heavy drinking.

What to drink: A half of Kronenbourg or a glass of wine. The upstairs dining room serves excellent French food.

11. The Dog and Duck (1734)

Address: 18 Bateman Street, W1D 3AJ

A beautiful small pub in Soho with ornate Victorian tiles and mirrors. George Orwell was a regular, and John Constable lived on the same street. The name refers to the sport of duck hunting with dogs that took place in the fields that are now Soho Square. The tiny interior is one of the most beautiful pub interiors in London.

What to drink: Timothy Taylor's Landlord, one of the best cask ales available anywhere.

12. The Salisbury (1892)

Address: 90 St Martin's Lane, WC2N 4AP

A spectacular late Victorian gin palace near the West End theatres. The interior is a riot of cut glass, bronze nymphs, Art Nouveau light fittings, and ornate mirrors. It is one of the best-preserved examples of a grand Victorian pub in London and a wonderful spot for a pre-theatre drink.

What to drink: A gin and tonic in honour of the gin palace tradition, while admiring the extraordinary interior.

Bloomsbury, Holborn, and Clerkenwell

13. The Museum Tavern (1723)

Address: 49 Great Russell Street, WC1B 3BA

Directly opposite the British Museum, this pub has been a favourite of scholars and intellectuals for three centuries. Karl Marx reportedly drank here while researching Das Kapital at the British Museum reading room across the road. Arthur Conan Doyle was also a patron.

What to drink: A pint of cask ale at a window table, watching the museum crowds go by.

14. The Princess Louise (1872)

Address: 208 High Holborn, WC1V 7EP

One of the most beautiful pub interiors in Britain. Grade II listed, with original etched mirrors, ornate tiling, and wooden partitioned snug areas that were once used to separate different social classes. Named after Queen Victoria's fourth daughter. The restoration is immaculate.

What to drink: A pint of Sam Smith's at impressively low prices for central London — around 4 pounds.

15. The Eagle (1991, building from 1880s)

Address: 159 Farringdon Road, EC1R 3AL

While the building is Victorian, The Eagle is historic for a different reason: it was the first gastropub, opening in 1991 and revolutionising the London pub food scene forever. Before The Eagle, pub food meant crisps and maybe a sad sandwich. The steak sandwich here changed everything. It is the birthplace of a movement that transformed British food culture.

What to drink: Whatever is on the changing menu — the food is still the main draw.

West London and Beyond

16. The Churchill Arms (1750)

Address: 119 Kensington Church Street, W8 7LN

Famous for its extraordinary exterior covered in flowers and hanging baskets — over 100 window boxes and baskets in summer, and 90 Christmas trees in winter. Winston Churchill's grandparents were regular customers, hence the name. Inside, the pub is packed with wartime memorabilia and curiosities. The Thai kitchen at the back serves excellent, affordable food.

What to drink: A Fuller's London Pride, followed by pad thai from the Thai kitchen. One of the best-value meals in Kensington.

17. The Windsor Castle (1835)

Address: 114 Campden Hill Road, W8 7AR

A charming pub with a large walled garden, rare dark wood partitions dividing the bar into separate rooms, and a history of bohemian clientele. The partitions are Grade II listed and among the finest surviving examples in London. The garden is one of the best pub gardens in the city.

What to drink: A pint in the walled garden when the weather allows.

18. The Spaniards Inn (1585)

Address: Spaniards Road, NW3 7JJ

Perched on the edge of Hampstead Heath, this 16th-century pub claims Dick Turpin the highwayman as a former patron — his father was allegedly the landlord. Keats wrote Ode to a Nightingale nearby, Dickens used it as a setting in The Pickwick Papers, and Bram Stoker mentions it in Dracula. The pub garden is gorgeous in summer.

What to drink: Walk across Hampstead Heath first, then reward yourself with a pint of cask ale in the garden.

19. The Holly Bush (1807, building from 1643)

Address: 22 Holly Mount, NW3 6SG

Hidden up a steep hill in Hampstead Village, this pub occupies a building that was originally the stable block for the painter George Romney's house. The gas lighting gives it an incredibly atmospheric glow in the evening. The snug rooms, real fires in winter, and old wooden settles make it feel like stepping back in time.

What to drink: A pint by the fire in winter or in the small terrace garden in summer. The roast dinners on Sundays are excellent.

20. The Mayflower (1620)

Address: 117 Rotherhithe Street, SE16 4NF

Named after the ship that carried the Pilgrim Fathers to America in 1620, this riverside pub in Rotherhithe is where Captain Christopher Jones moored the Mayflower before the voyage. It is the only pub in England licensed to sell both British and American postage stamps. The back deck overhangs the Thames, and at high tide the water laps just below your feet.

What to drink: A pint on the back deck watching the Thames flow past. On a clear evening, the views upriver towards Tower Bridge are superb.

Pub Crawl Planning Tips

  • Start early. Many historic pubs open at 11 AM or noon. The atmosphere is often better in the afternoon before the after-work crowds arrive.
  • Eat along the way. Several of these pubs serve excellent food. The Eagle, The Churchill Arms, and The George Inn all have good kitchens.
  • Do not try all 20 in one day. Split the crawl into manageable sections: East London, Southwark, Central, and the northern pubs.
  • Try cask ale. London's historic pubs are the best places to drink real ale, served from the cask rather than pressurized kegs. Ask the bar staff for a recommendation.
  • Visit midweek. These pubs get very crowded on Friday and Saturday evenings. A Tuesday or Wednesday afternoon visit lets you appreciate the interiors in peace.
  • Look up. Many pubs have beautiful original ceilings, light fittings, and plasterwork that people miss because they are focused on the bar.

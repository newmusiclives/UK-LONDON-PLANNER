7 Wonderfully English Traditions Americans Will Love (A St George's Day Field Guide)

Published 23 April 2026 — St George's Day

Today is St George's Day — April 23rd — and there's a decent chance you've never heard of it. Don't worry. A solid percentage of English people forgot about it too. England's national day is, fittingly, the most understated national day on Earth. No fireworks. No parade down the Mall. Just a quiet flag on a few pubs and maybe a vicar mentioning it during announcements.

That underselling is actually the most English thing about it, and it tells you everything you need to know about visiting this country. England's best stuff isn't announced with a megaphone. It's embedded in the way people queue, drink tea, and say sorry to a door they walked into.

If you're an American planning your first trip to England — or your fifth — consider this your field guide to the unwritten rules, odd rituals, and quietly wonderful customs that nobody puts on the tourism posters. Know these, and you'll go from tourist to temporary local in about forty-eight hours.

1. The Pub Round System (This Will Save Your Social Life)

Here's how it works. Someone in your group goes to the bar and buys drinks for everyone. Then someone else does. Then someone else. You never buy just your own drink. You never split the tab after each order. You never — ever — skip your turn.

Americans often try to settle up individually or, worse, offer to Venmo someone. Both are social crimes roughly on par with cutting in line at Disneyland.

The system looks inefficient. It is inefficient. That's not the point. The point is that buying a round is a small act of trust — "I'll get this one because I know you'll get the next" — and it oils the wheels of an entire evening. At pubs like The Lamb and Flag in Covent Garden (33 Rose Street, WC2E 9EB) or Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese on Fleet Street (145 Fleet Street, EC4A 2BU), rounds have been flowing since before America existed as a country. Walk in, offer to buy the first round, and you'll make friends faster than any icebreaker app has ever managed.

Tip: If someone says "what are you having?" they're offering to buy you a drink. The correct response is to tell them what you'd like, not to say "oh no, I couldn't possibly." That just makes everything awkward.

2. The Apology Reflex

English people apologise constantly and to everything. Someone steps on your foot? They'll apologise. You'll also apologise. A stranger bumps your suitcase in the Tube? Both of you will mutter "sorry" as if you've just committed a minor war crime.

This extends to inanimate objects. Walk into a lamp post in Marylebone and there's a non-zero chance you'll hear yourself say "oh, sorry" before your brain catches up. It happened to me on Marylebone High Street last October and a woman across the road nodded sympathetically, as if the lamp post had feelings worth considering.

"Sorry" in England doesn't actually mean "I apologise." It means approximately fifteen different things — excuse me, pardon, I acknowledge your existence, please don't be upset, I'm British and this is just what we do. Learn to deploy it freely and you'll blend in beautifully.

3. Cream Tea (And the Devon vs. Cornwall War You Didn't Know About)

A cream tea is a scone served with clotted cream and jam, alongside a pot of tea. Simple. Except for the question that has divided the West Country for centuries: cream first, or jam first?

In Devon, you spread the cream onto the scone first, then add jam on top. In Cornwall, it's jam first, cream on top. Both sides feel very strongly about this. Marriages have been strained.

In London, nobody cares which order you use, but they will care if you call it a "biscuit." It's a scone. Pronounced either "skonn" (rhymes with gone) or "skoan" (rhymes with phone), depending on who you ask, which is yet another argument nobody has settled.

For a proper cream tea in London, The Orangery at Kensington Palace (Kensington Gardens, W8 4PX) does a solid version from around £22. Or for something less palace-adjacent, Buns & Buns in Covent Garden (1 Victoria Embankment, WC2R 2AB) does scones that don't mess about.

4. The Queue

The English queue is not a line. It is a civic institution. A silent contract between strangers that civilisation has not collapsed.

There is no cutting. There is no saving spots for four friends who went to the loo. There is no pretending you didn't see the queue. At bus stops where there is physically no way to form a straight line, everyone still somehow knows whose turn it is. It's genuinely supernatural.

The penalty for queue-jumping is not confrontation — that would be too direct. Instead, someone behind you will audibly sigh, and another person will say "I think there's actually a queue" in a tone that could freeze magma. You will feel shame you didn't know was possible.

Join the queue. Enjoy the queue. The queue is where the English do some of their best quiet bonding — commiserating about weather, tutting at someone's phone volume, forming temporary alliances against anyone who looks like they might skip ahead.

5. The Sunday Roast

Every Sunday, a significant portion of England sits down to a plate of roast meat, roast potatoes, at least two veg, a Yorkshire pudding (a savoury batter puff, not a dessert — American visitors get confused by this roughly once every eleven seconds), and enough gravy to fill a small canal.

This is non-negotiable. Even people who barely cook will attempt a roast on Sunday. Pubs that get the roast right develop cult followings. The Anchor & Hope in Waterloo (36 The Cut, SE1 8LP) has been slinging some of London's best roasts for years — get there early because they don't take reservations and the queue (see point 4) gets serious. The Harwood Arms in Fulham (Walham Grove, SW6 1QP) earned a Michelin star partly on the strength of its Sunday offering.

If you're in London over a Sunday and you eat anything other than a roast for lunch, you've missed something fundamental.

6. Morris Dancing (Yes, Really)

Grown adults. Bells strapped to their shins. Sticks or handkerchiefs. Accordion music. Coordinated hopping. In public. On purpose.

Morris dancing looks, to the uninitiated, like a Renaissance Faire lost a bet. But it dates back to at least the 15th century, and it's performed in earnest across England every spring — especially around St George's Day and May Day. You'll occasionally spot a Morris side (that's what the groups are called) performing outside pubs in places like Richmond Green or at folk festivals in the Cotswolds.

Do not laugh. Well, you can laugh a bit. Even the dancers know it looks absurd. But there's something genuinely moving about a tradition that has survived six centuries purely on the basis that people kept showing up and doing it. No government grant. No branding. Just bells and sticks and stubbornness.

7. The Weather Conversation

English people don't talk about the weather because they're boring. They talk about the weather because it's the universal social lubricant — a safe topic that allows two strangers to have a conversation without the terrifying possibility that it might become personal.

"Bit grey today." "At least it's not raining." "They said it'd clear up by Thursday." These are not meteorological observations. They're social handshakes. The correct response is always agreement, plus a slight escalation: "Yeah, could do with a bit of sun, couldn't we?"

In April, you'll get to deploy this approximately forty times per day, because English spring weather changes every twenty minutes. You can experience sunshine, drizzle, wind, and something that feels like mild hail all before lunch. This is considered normal. Pack layers.

The Deeper Point

St George's Day isn't loud because England's identity doesn't live in grand declarations. It lives in these small, specific, slightly ridiculous rituals — the round at the pub, the queue at the bus stop, the argument about scone pronunciation, the apology to a lamp post. That's where the country actually is.

And honestly? That's what makes it such a phenomenal place to visit. The stuff worth knowing isn't in the guidebook. It's in the rhythm.
